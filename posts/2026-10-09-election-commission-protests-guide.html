Heading to a protest this week? What you need to know about the Election Commission protests

A plain guide to the protests against CEC Gyanesh Kumar: what's coming up, what restrictions are in force, where the case stands, and your rights if detained.

By Prabuddha · 9 October 2026 · Reporting as of publication

Protests against Chief Election Commissioner Gyanesh Kumar have spread from New Delhi to Mumbai and to district towns over the past three weeks. Here is what you need to know before you step out.

Why people are protesting

The protests centre on a nationwide revision of the electoral rolls. Critics say millions of eligible voters were removed and that the changes favour the ruling BJP. The Election Commission denies this and says it removed only duplicate, deceased or ineligible entries. Kumar has not commented publicly.

The pressure grew after the Indian Express reported that the Commission's two deputies had raised concerns about the voter-list revision at least 14 times over 10 months.

What's coming up

  • Saturday, 10 October, New Delhi: The Cockroach Janta Party (CJP) has called for a mass youth mobilisation at Jantar Mantar. The Gen Z movement began as online satire in May, and its July protests preceded the education minister's resignation.
  • INDIA bloc campaign: Opposition MPs marched from Parliament to the Election Commission's office at Nirvachan Sadan on 6 October as part of a "Save Democracy" campaign. District-level padyatras and meetings were planned until 8 October. Watch for further dates.

What restrictions are in force

  • Section 163 in New Delhi: Delhi Police have imposed Section 163 of the BNSS. It bars gatherings of more than five people and bans slogans and speeches in the affected area.
  • Detentions: On 2 October, more than 500 people were reportedly detained near Jantar Mantar, including former Delhi Chief Minister Atishi. At least 11 metro stations were closed. Plan your route and a way home.
  • Barricades: Multi-layered barricades blocked the roads to Nirvachan Sadan during the MPs' march on 6 October.
  • Notices before travel: In Ballarpur, Maharashtra, police sent some CJP volunteers notices asking why they planned to attend the 10 October protest and who was paying for their travel. The CJP has called this intimidation.

Safety concerns

Three women journalists have accused Delhi Police officers of sexual harassment at the 3 October protest. Police say they will hold a fair and impartial inquiry. Earlier, Amnesty International reported that authorities used lethal weapons against crowds during the July protests.

Where the case stands

A Supreme Court bench led by Chief Justice Surya Kant has said it will examine the voter-roll decisions and annul any found to be contrary to law. This is the most important development to follow.

If permission is granted

When Delhi Police allowed a CJP sit-in at Jantar Mantar in June, they applied the conditions of the Supreme Court's July 2018 order on protests there: keep it peaceful, don't block traffic, avoid any damage to property, and keep to the permitted hours. Breaking these can lead to the permission being cancelled. Expect similar terms again.

Your rights if detained

This is the general legal position, not legal advice.

  • You have the right to be told why you are being arrested.
  • You have the right to consult a lawyer of your choice.
  • You must be produced before a magistrate within 24 hours (Article 22 of the Constitution).
Before you go: carry ID, write a lawyer's or legal-aid number on paper, charge your phone, tell someone your plans, and go with people you trust.
